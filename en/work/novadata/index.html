NovaData Insights

Using People Analytics to diagnose unwanted attrition and prioritise HR action

Academic team project · Simulated HR data
Role: Primary analytical and technical contributor
Main tools: Power BI · KNIME · Excel

Formal team assignment. I served as the primary analytical and technical contributor, with peer review and feedback from the other team members.

The challenge

NovaData Insights is a simulated analytics and AI consultancy experiencing rapid growth alongside increasing unwanted employee turnover.

The business model relied on tight cost control, below-market compensation and reduced investment in learning and development. At the same time, the organisation was showing signs of workload pressure, limited career progression and deteriorating employee experience.

The People Analytics challenge was to move beyond a headline turnover rate and answer three practical questions: Where was unwanted attrition concentrated? Which workforce factors were associated with it? And where should HR action be prioritised?

A secondary objective was to test whether historical employee data could support useful attrition-risk modelling.

How I approached it

I worked with a simulated multi-year HR dataset covering 2017–2025, with 649 employee-year records and information on organisational structure, tenure, compensation, performance, potential, training, working hours, climate, engagement and employee exits.

I structured the analysis in three layers.

First, I developed Power BI dashboards to understand workforce composition, attrition patterns, employee experience and the estimated economic impact of turnover.

Second, I used statistical analysis to test whether the patterns visible in the dashboards were supported by associations in the historical data. This included continuous, ordinal and categorical variables.

Finally, I implemented and compared several classification models in KNIME to assess whether the available data contained enough predictive signal to support an unwanted-attrition risk score.

The objective was not simply to build a model, but to connect HR data → evidence → business interpretation → action.

What the data showed

In 2025, the simulated organisation recorded 62 exits, including 55 unwanted exits and 51 classified as avoidable. Unwanted attrition reached approximately 34%, 4.4 percentage points higher than the previous year.

Descriptively, unwanted exits were particularly concentrated in Data Science and Marketing and among employees with approximately 2–5 years of tenure. Burnout and career development were the most frequently recorded primary exit reasons.

The employee-experience data also pointed to internal pressure. Average engagement was approximately 2.71/5, overall climate 2.82/5, and recorded weekly working hours averaged 46.6 hours. The average salary gap against market benchmarks was around −14%.

Workload manageability and deadline reasonableness were among the weakest climate dimensions.

The historical analysis identified statistically significant—but generally small—associations between unwanted attrition and variables including engagement, workload, climate, deadlines, training, career opportunities and perceived pay fairness.

Importantly, I separated where attrition appeared to concentrate from which variables had stronger analytical evidence of association. Department and role were useful for segmentation, but did not show significant associations in the corresponding tests.

From analysis to HR action

I translated the findings into a set of HR priorities rather than treating every statistically significant variable as equally actionable.

The strongest themes were workload and capacity management, clearer career development, protected learning opportunities and compensation fairness.

I also identified several improvements to the People Analytics infrastructure itself: stronger measurement of employee experience, better capture of reasons for leaving, psychosocial-risk assessment and more consistent data for multi-year analysis.

To connect workforce outcomes with business impact, I also modelled the potential cost of turnover using assumptions around recruitment, vacancy time, onboarding, ramp-up and productivity.

For 2017–2024, the scenario produced an estimated turnover-related impact of approximately €2.58M, most of it associated with unwanted exits. I treated this explicitly as a scenario-based estimate, not observed accounting expenditure.

What the model could — and could not — tell us

I compared logistic regression, decision tree and random forest models, including alternative ways of representing organisational climate.

The best-performing specification was a logistic regression using individual climate dimensions. It achieved a ROC AUC of approximately .63, but precision for unwanted attrition was only around 11%.

That result mattered more than simply having a predictive model.

The model detected some signal, but its performance was not strong enough to support reliable employee-level decisions. A large proportion of employees classified as high risk would be false positives.

I therefore treated the 2025 scores as an exploratory prioritisation tool, not as a system for predicting which specific employees would leave.

This was one of the main conclusions of the project: analytical sophistication does not compensate for insufficient predictive performance or limited data quality.

My contribution

I led and executed the analytical work end to end: data preparation, Power BI development, data modelling and DAX, statistical analysis, KNIME workflows, predictive modelling, risk scoring, turnover-cost modelling, interpretation of results, HR recommendations, and preparation of the final report and executive presentation.

The project was formally submitted as a team assignment, with the other team members reviewing the work and providing feedback.

Limitations

This case uses simulated HR data, so its numerical findings should not be interpreted as evidence about a real organisation.

The analysis was observational and therefore does not establish causal relationships. Climate and engagement data were unavailable before 2022, some employees had repeated observations across years, and the number of unwanted exits available for modelling was limited.

The predictive model showed only modest discrimination and low precision, while the turnover-cost analysis depended on assumptions defined for the case.

These limitations were incorporated into the interpretation rather than treated as secondary caveats.

Technical notes

For the predictive analysis, historical data from 2017–2024 were divided using a stratified 75/25 train-test split, with SMOTE applied only to the training partition.

The classification target distinguished unwanted attrition from all other observations; the negative class therefore included both employees who stayed and employees whose exits were classified as desired.

The split was performed at the employee-year record level rather than grouped by employee. Because some employees had repeated observations across years, this is an additional methodological limitation.

The selected logistic model was subsequently applied to the 162 employee records from 2025 to generate exploratory risk scores for visualisation in Power BI.
