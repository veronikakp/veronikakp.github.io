01 / Recruitment & Selection · Mini-case

Designing a Competency-Based Recruitment & Selection Process

Talent Acquisition · Competency-Based Selection

Academic team project · I led the overall structuring and design of the competency-based selection process, coordinating and integrating contributions from the other team members.

Designed as an academic selection process · Not implemented with real candidates

The challenge

The project was built around a provincial fundraising manager role and focused on one practical question: how can job requirements be translated into a structured, competency-based selection process in which each assessment stage has a clear purpose?

The starting point was the role profile. We distinguished between job-specific and organizational competencies, defined the required level for each competency and identified which requirements were critical to the selection decision. This created a common reference point for attraction, screening, assessment and final decision-making rather than treating each stage as a separate exercise.

End-to-end selection architecture

I led the design of the overall process and coordinated how the different contributions were integrated into a single selection architecture:

The recruitment stage translated the role profile into candidate-facing requirements. CV screening then narrowed the pool against predefined criteria before candidates progressed to the assessment stages. The design combined several sources of evidence so that the decision would not depend on a single test, interview or evaluator impression.

External instruments such as BAC and COMPETEA were selected as part of the academic design and are referenced only at methodological level. Proprietary items, scoring norms, test materials and other protected content are not reproduced.

From competencies to assessment evidence

The central artefact of the project was a competency–assessment matrix connecting each target competency with the methods designed to assess it. Depending on the competency, evidence could come from standardized assessment, behavioural observation during the group exercise, the structured interview, or a combination of methods.

This was an important design principle: assessment methods were selected because they generated relevant evidence for defined competency requirements, not simply because they were commonly used selection techniques.

The group exercise therefore included behavioural indicators linked to the competencies being observed. The structured interview followed the same logic, with questions designed around specific competency requirements so candidate responses could be assessed against the role rather than through an unstructured conversation.

Evidence integration and decision logic

The final stage consolidated information from the different assessments into a candidate-evaluation framework. The aim was to compare evidence across the process and support a more systematic final decision while retaining visibility over where each conclusion came from.

The project demonstrates my ability to structure end-to-end competency-based Talent Acquisition: understanding the target profile, translating requirements into an assessment strategy, coordinating multiple assessment methods and integrating evidence for a final selection decision.

It does not represent a live recruitment campaign. No real candidates were assessed, no hiring decisions were made, and the proposed evaluation framework was not empirically validated for predictive accuracy.

02 / Organizational Network Analysis · Compact mini-case

Using Organizational Network Analysis to Inform Team Coordination

Organizational Development · Relational Team Diagnosis

Individual reconstruction and re-analysis of an academic Master's case originally developed as a team assignment.

No new organizational data were collected.

Tools: Python · pandas · NetworkX · Gephi

The question

The case examined the relational structure of a small healthcare team using a directed network of interpersonal ratings. I independently reconstructed the original matrix, reviewed the coding and re-ran the analysis to examine patterns of positive and negative relations, reciprocity, bridging positions and relational tensions.

The aim was not to turn network metrics into conclusions about people. It was to use relational data as one input into an organizational diagnosis and identify what should be explored further before intervention.

What the network showed

The reconstructed case contained 11 actors and 110 directed ratings. The positive network had a density of 33.6%, the negative network 15.5%, and positive reciprocity was 59.5%.

The analysis highlighted people occupying stronger bridging positions in the positive network and identified where negative relations were concentrated. It also flagged 39 ratings whose interpretation was ambiguous and therefore required validation before being used in any intervention decision. Positive and negative networks were kept separate rather than treating the original categories as a single scale.

From network pattern to organizational hypothesis

The central principle of the re-analysis was to distinguish observed network structure from causal explanation.

A bridging position can suggest a possible informal facilitator, but it does not prove leadership. Reciprocal negative ties can indicate a relationship that deserves attention, but they do not establish responsibility, misconduct or harassment. Likewise, interpersonal affinity is not the same as actual coordination, information flow, trust or professional collaboration.

The analysis therefore used in-degree, reciprocity, betweenness and dyadic discrepancies descriptively, with sensitivity checks where the original case information was ambiguous.

From diagnosis to OD action

The resulting logic was:

relational data → network structure → cautious interpretation → validation needs → targeted OD action

Before intervention, the case proposed clarifying uncertain relationships, examining how coordination actually worked and identifying the nature of confirmed conflicts. Actions would then depend on what was verified: communication and coordination agreements, conflict-management measures, or formal procedures where inappropriate conduct or possible harassment required investigation.

A follow-up stage would reassess both agreed working arrangements and relational patterns after implementation.

The case shows how I use relational analytics to support Organizational Development without overstating what the data can establish.

Observed pattern → hypothesis → validation needed

Validate → Act on confirmed issues → Reassess

03 / Organizational Climate · Compact mini-case

From Organizational Climate Diagnosis to HR Action

Employee Experience · Organizational Climate

Academic team project · Simulated organizational case · Project lead

I led the overall project and independently developed the proposed data-collection process, the simulated results and their diagnostic presentation. The HR action plan was subsequently discussed and agreed collaboratively with the team.

Measurement plan

The project was designed as a complete organizational-climate assessment process, rather than as a standalone survey.

The proposed measurement strategy combined the Work Environment Scale (WES), targeted interviews and exit interviews, supported by a communication and participation plan. The WES component covered 90 items across 10 climate dimensions grouped into three broader factors. Targeted interviews were intended to investigate priority areas emerging from the survey, while exit interviews added another source of information on the simulated retention problem.

The WES is referenced only at methodological level. Proprietary items, scoring materials, norms and other protected content are not reproduced.

Simulated diagnostic findings

Because this was a fully simulated organizational case, no employee responses were collected.

I constructed the simulated results and developed their diagnostic presentation to show how a climate profile could be interpreted across dimensions. The aim was to move from a large set of responses to a concise view of relative strengths, weaker areas and priorities for further investigation.

The simulated diagnosis highlighted weaker areas around involvement, autonomy and innovation, alongside comparatively stronger clarity.

From diagnosis to HR priorities

The project connected climate findings to action areas rather than stopping at measurement. Priority dimensions were translated into proposals around leadership and feedback, autonomy and work organization, and selected people practices such as onboarding and employee development.

The full HR action plan was subsequently discussed and agreed collaboratively within the team. The portfolio version highlights only the actions most directly connected to the diagnostic priorities rather than reproducing the complete academic plan. The original exercise developed a broader set of HR actions and incorporated responsibilities, timing and follow-up.

It does not represent a real client engagement, real employee data or implemented interventions, and no organizational outcomes are claimed.

04 / Questionnaire Prototype · Card

From Research Evidence to a Questionnaire Prototype

People Measurement · Questionnaire Design

Individual academic project

The project explored how research evidence on group contexts associated with escalation of commitment could be translated into a structured questionnaire prototype.

I moved from the literature to a construct map, then defined dimensions, indicators and items so that an abstract concept could be represented through observable questions. The final prototype contained 25 items using a six-point response scale, including both directly keyed and reverse-keyed items.

The core capability demonstrated is construct operationalisation:

research evidence → construct → dimensions → indicators → items → questionnaire prototype

The project was designed as an academic measurement exercise, not as a validated assessment tool. It did not include psychometric validation, factor analysis, reliability testing or empirically established cut-off scores, and it should not be interpreted as a diagnostic instrument.
